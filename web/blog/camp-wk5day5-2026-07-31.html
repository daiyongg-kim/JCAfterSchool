The Purple Water That Tells the Truth 🧪
양배추 물의 비밀

JC After School · Coquitlam, BC · July 31, 2026 (Fri)
Science Day 🔬

This is purple water made from soaking purple (“red”) cabbage in water. We used it for a science experiment today. On its own it looks like nothing more than a jug of coloured water — but this particular purple has a job to do. 💜

The purple liquid from the cabbage is of a neutral pH, and it is able to react with any liquid substance and cause a change in colour according to the acidity of that liquid. Drop a little into something acidic and it turns red. Drop it into something alkaline and it turns blue, then green. The liquid tells on itself. 🧐

🧪 The pH scale
ACIDpH 0 – 6
NEUTRALpH 7
BASEpH 8 – 14

pH is a scale that measures how acidic or base a liquid is, running from 0 to 14, with 7 being neutral — the pH of pure water, H₂O. (pH stands for the hydrogen-releasing potential of a liquid.)

🫙 Setting Up the Lab Jars, bottles and a colour chart

A row of clean glass jars, a table of liquids to test, and a printed indicator chart to compare against — pale pink at the acidic end, through purple in the middle, to blue, green and yellow at the alkaline end. Everything ready before a single drop was poured. 🧫

And every scientist needs a record sheet. Theirs had three columns: the chemical liquid solution · the colour it turned when mixed with cabbage water · the approximate pH, with a box to tick for acid or base. 📝

🌈 Pour, Watch, Record One liquid at a time

Then the work began. A little cabbage water into each jar, a careful look, a comparison against the chart, and a number written down. Milk barely moved off neutral. Lemon juice shot straight to the acid end. Coffee, vinegar, perfume — each one gave itself away. 🔍

And the finished sheets tell the story better than we can. Milk ≈ 6 · Lemon 2 · Coffee 5 · Vinegar 3 · Perfume 5–6 — all reds and pinks, all acids. Then over on the other sheet: baking soda, dish soap, laundry soap, glass cleaner, floor cleaner — every single one drawn in blue. 🔵

🍋 The Moment of the Day Lemon juice goes bright red

Here it is: pure lemon juice, being acidic, changed to bright red the moment a little purple cabbage water was added. 😮😀🧐

At the other end of the scale, the cleaners went the opposite way entirely — straight past blue toward green, and the most extreme ones further still, into a bright orange-yellow. Same purple water, opposite answer. 🔵

⚠️ Safety first, always
  • The younger children only handled safe products — coffee, milk, lemon juice, perfume, purple cabbage and baking soda.
  • Only the older students handled the dish soap and the glass and floor cleaner — and without actually touching any of it.
  • Anything corrosive was handled strictly by the teacher, kept in sealed jars far away from the students. The more extreme alkaline products were kept away from the children entirely.

The conclusion, reached by the little scientists and the middle school science experts alike: liquids can be acidic, neutral, or alkaline (base). Depending on their pH, they will turn either red or blue — and reveal their acidity level by the colour they change to, regardless of their original colour. 💡
